Allergen Control - Preventing Cross-Contact and Ensuring Accurate Labeling

Food allergen controls are one of the four preventive control types under FSMA. They require a facility to identify the major food allergens in its products, prevent unintended cross-contact during receiving, storage, processing, and rework, and make sure every finished food label declares the allergens it contains. A single labeling error or missed changeover can send an allergic consumer to hospital and trigger a recall. This map follows the pathway from allergen identification through control design, implementation, and verification.

Major Food Allergens

The nine allergens FDA regulates as major: milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans, and sesame.

Basis: FALCPA and FASTER Act

Cross-Contact

The unintentional incorporation of a food allergen into a food that is not intended to contain it.

Basis: Equipment, people, and rework

Allergen Controls

Segregation, scheduling, cleaning, and labeling procedures that prevent cross-contact and misbranding.

Basis: Written and verified procedures

Label Accuracy

Every major allergen present, including those from sub-ingredients and rework, declared on the finished food label.

Basis: Label review and reconciliation

Major Food Allergens (Overview)

The Original Eight

Milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, and soybeans, defined by FALCPA 2004.

FD&C Act 201(qq)

Sesame - the Ninth Allergen

Sesame became a major food allergen under the FASTER Act of 2021, with labeling required from 1 January 2023.

FASTER Act 2021

Naming the Source

Fish, crustacean shellfish, and tree nut labels must name the specific type, such as salmon, shrimp, or almond.

FD&C Act 403(w)

Allergen Changeover Sequence (Overview)

Stop and remove Production stops, product and ingredients from the previous run are removed, and the line is secured for cleaning.
Clean to the defined method Equipment is cleaned using the written method, with dry or wet cleaning matched to the equipment and the allergen involved.
Verify clean Visual inspection plus swab or other testing, as the plan requires, confirms no allergen residue remains before the line is released.
Release and record The line is released for the next product, the correct label is confirmed, and the changeover is documented and signed.

Key Allergen Control Outputs (Overview)

Allergen matrix A current view of which allergens are present in which ingredients, lines, and products.
Cleaning and verification records Evidence that each allergen changeover followed the method and that the result was confirmed.
Label control records Label review, line start-up checks, and reconciliation showing the right label went on the right product.
Reanalysis record Documented review after changes, confirming the controls remain adequate or recording what changed.

Allergen Control Quality Elements

Complete Allergen Inventory Every ingredient, sub-ingredient, processing aid, and rework stream has a confirmed allergen status.
Accurate Allergen Matrix Matrix kept current and tied to lines, equipment, and products so decisions are made from facts.
Segregation Discipline Dedicated tools, separate storage, and scheduling rules that people follow without exceptions.
Cleaning Effectiveness Methods proven in practice to remove allergen residue from the specific equipment they are used on.
Label Accuracy Every label matches the formulation, with a check that catches errors before product ships.
Rework Control Rework containing allergens added only to products that declare them, with clear identification and records.
Staff Awareness Operators, sanitation, and warehouse staff understand allergens and know when to stop and escalate.
Change Management Formulation, supplier, equipment, and label changes trigger an allergen review before they go live.

Never do this

Assume a changeover is clean without checking. Add allergen-containing rework to a product that does not declare it. Print or apply labels without reconciling them to the product. Accept a supplier ingredient change without reviewing its allergen status. Rely on a "may contain" statement in place of controls.

Cross-contact vs cross-contamination

Cross-contact is the unintentional transfer of an allergen to a food that is not meant to contain it. Cross-contamination usually refers to the transfer of microorganisms. A facility needs controls for both, but the verification tools and cleaning methods differ.

Precautionary labeling is not a control

A statement such as "may contain" is voluntary and does not replace good manufacturing practice. FDA expects allergen cross-contact to be controlled, and the statement should not be used to cover for poor controls or labeling errors.
