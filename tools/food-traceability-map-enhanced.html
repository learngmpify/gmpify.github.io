Food Traceability - Tracking Listed Foods One Step Back and One Step Forward

The Food Traceability Rule requires anyone who manufactures, processes, packs, or holds foods on the FDA Food Traceability List to keep additional records at defined critical tracking events and to pass key data elements to the next party in the supply chain. A traceability lot code links the records, and the facility must be able to give FDA an electronic sortable spreadsheet within 24 hours during an outbreak or recall. This map follows the pathway from scoping and planning through lot code assignment, record capture, sharing, and readiness.

21 CFR 1 Subpart S
FSMA Section 204
Traceability Lot Code

Food Traceability List

The foods for which additional traceability records are required, including foods that contain a listed food in its listed form.

Basis: Scope of the rule

Critical Tracking Event

A supply-chain event, such as harvesting, cooling, initial packing, first land-based receiving, shipping, receiving, or transformation.

Basis: Where records are made

Key Data Element

Information tied to a critical tracking event that must be maintained and, in some cases, provided to the next party.

Basis: What is recorded

Traceability Lot Code

A descriptor, often alphanumeric, that uniquely identifies a traceability lot and links the records.

Basis: Assigned at set events

Critical Tracking Events (Overview)

Origin Events

Harvesting, cooling, initial packing, and first land-based receiving of fish, where lots are created and the first records are made.

21 CFR 1.1325 to 1.1335

Movement Events

Shipping, where key data elements go to the next recipient, and receiving, where the previous source and lot are recorded.

21 CFR 1.1340 · 1.1345

Transformation

A new lot is created when food is changed, with the input lots and the new output lot linked in the records.

21 CFR 1.1350

Outbreak or Recall Record Request (Overview)

Receive and scope The request identifies the foods and date ranges, or specific lot codes, and the time FDA expects the records.
Locate the records Records are found, wherever stored, including those held by another entity that keeps them for you.
Produce within 24 hours Records and the information needed to understand them reach FDA within 24 hours, or a reasonable time FDA agrees to.
Provide the spreadsheet An electronic sortable spreadsheet of the relevant records is supplied, unless the entity qualifies for an exception.

Key Traceability Outputs (Overview)

Traceability plan Written plan covering procedures, listed foods, lot code assignment, a point of contact, and a farm map where needed.
Key data element records Records for each critical tracking event, tied to a traceability lot code.
Lot code assignment records Evidence of when and where each traceability lot code was assigned.
Electronic sortable spreadsheet A ready method to provide relevant records to FDA in a sortable format within 24 hours.

Traceability Quality Elements

Clear Scope Listed foods, exemptions, and the basis for each decision are documented and reviewed.
Documented Plan A written plan that meets the required contents and is kept current.
Consistent Lot Codes Lot codes assigned at the right events, in a consistent format, and never at events that should not create a lot.
Complete Key Data Elements Every required element captured for each event, not just the ones that are convenient.
Partner Data Exchange Reliable, timely exchange of key data elements with suppliers and customers.
Record Integrity Records legible, protected from loss, and linked by lot so the chain does not break.
24-Hour Retrieval Practiced ability to locate and produce records and a sortable spreadsheet within 24 hours.
Change Management Plan and records updated when operations, partners, or the Food Traceability List change.

Regulatory Text - Source Provisions

21 CFR 1.1320 (paraphrased - refer to the official regulation for exact wording)

A traceability lot code must be assigned when initially packing a raw agricultural commodity, when performing the first land-based receiving of food obtained from a fishing vessel, and when transforming a food on the Food Traceability List. A new traceability lot code should not be created for other activities, such as shipping.

21 CFR 1.1340 (paraphrased - refer to the official regulation for exact wording)

When shipping a food on the Food Traceability List, the shipper must maintain records containing the traceability lot code, the quantity and unit of measure, the product description, the recipient location, the shipping location, the ship date, the traceability lot code source, and a reference document, and must provide the required information to the immediate subsequent recipient.

21 CFR 1.1455(c) (paraphrased - refer to the official regulation for exact wording)

Records and the information needed to understand them must be provided to FDA within 24 hours of a request, or within a reasonable time FDA agrees to, and for outbreaks, recalls, and other public health threats the information must be provided in an electronic sortable spreadsheet, unless an exception applies.

Anticipated Implementation Gaps - Food Traceability

Illustrative gaps to anticipate, based on the requirements of the rule. FDA enforcement of this rule has not yet begun, so these are not drawn from FDA citations.
No written traceability plan, or a plan missing required contents The plan is absent, or it lacks a point of contact, identification of listed foods, or a farm map where one is required.
Lot codes assigned at the wrong events A new code is created at shipping, or no new code is assigned at transformation, so the chain of lots is broken.
Key data elements missing from shipping or receiving records Records lack the traceability lot code source, the reference document, or the immediate previous source.
Records that cannot be produced in 24 hours or sorted Records are scattered across systems or held in formats that cannot be turned quickly into a sortable spreadsheet.

Best Practice Guidance

Standardize your lot code format One consistent format across lines and sites makes lots easier to link and records easier to search.
Exchange data with partners in a common format Agree with suppliers and customers on which elements move, in what format, and by when.
Run a mock 24-hour request Pick a finished lot, trace it back and forward, and time how long it takes to assemble the records.
Train receiving and shipping staff on the elements The people who handle the paperwork need to know exactly which elements each event requires.

Never do this

Create a new lot code when you only ship food. Combine lots without recording the link at transformation. Assume a partner has your records without confirming you can get them within 24 hours. Treat an exemption as permanent without documenting the basis. Hold records in formats that cannot be sorted or searched.

KDEs, CTEs, and TLCs

Critical tracking events are the moments in the supply chain that create records. Key data elements are the specific information recorded at each event. The traceability lot code is the identifier that ties the records together from one event to the next.

Exemptions and timing

Examples include produce farms at or below $25,000 in average annual sales, small retail and restaurants at or below $250,000, and foods receiving a kill step with records. FDA originally set a January 2026 compliance date, and Congress has directed FDA not to enforce before 20 July 2028, so check FDA for current status.
