OOS Result Obtained - Investigation Triggered Immediately Under 21 CFR 211.192

Any analytical result falling outside established acceptance criteria triggers mandatory investigation. This includes in-process tests, raw material testing and finished product release testing. No retesting may occur before investigation initiation. The two-phase framework below reflects FDA OOS Guidance October 2022, the Barr Laboratories court decision and current FDA inspection expectations.

21 CFR 211.192 - exact regulatory text

"All drug product production and control records, including those for packaging and labeling, shall be reviewed and approved by the quality control unit to determine compliance with all established, approved written procedures before a batch is released or distributed. Any unexplained discrepancy or the failure of a batch or any of its components to meet any of its specifications shall be thoroughly investigated, whether or not the batch has already been distributed."

⚠ Warning letter case - testing into compliance

Apotex 2025 warning letter: leak tests on critical aseptic equipment were repeated until passing results were obtained over 19 months. FDA cited this as testing into compliance - performing tests until a passing result is obtained is not an investigation. Every failing result is a signal requiring investigation, not a problem to be managed by repetition.

21 CFR 211.192  ·  21 CFR 211.165(f)  ·  FDA OOS Guidance October 2022  ·  Barr Laboratories Ltd v. Thompson, 2003
Phase I - Laboratory Investigation Complete within 3-10 business days
1Notify supervisor and QA unit immediately
Analyst reports OOS result to laboratory supervisor before any further action. QA unit notified. Original result preserved in raw data with no alterations. Documentation timestamped at point of discovery. The clock for Phase I starts at this point.
FDA OOS Guidance 2022 - Section IV.A

"The laboratory investigation should begin as soon as a suspect result is obtained. The laboratory supervisor should be notified and should be involved in any assessment of possible causes."

21 CFR 211.192  ·  21 CFR 211.194
↓
2Preserve all original materials and raw data
Retain the original test sample, reference standards, reagents, solutions and all instrument raw data files with audit trail intact. Do not discard any material until the investigation is complete and QA has approved the disposition decision.
✓ Best practice - data integrity

Print or electronically export instrument raw data files at this point and attach to the investigation record. If instrument access is later lost the raw data is already secured. FDA data integrity inspections look for gaps between electronic raw data and what appears in investigation records.

21 CFR 211.194  ·  ALCOA+ data integrity
↓
3Review original analytical procedure and execution
Laboratory supervisor reviews: calculation errors, instrument calibration status at time of analysis, standard preparation and certificate expiry, reagent expiry and preparation records, column condition and lot number, sample preparation technique against the validated method, environmental conditions during analysis and analyst training records for this specific method.
⚠ Warning letter case - analyst training gaps

Multiple 2024 and 2025 warning letters cited OOS investigations where the analyst who performed the original analysis was not confirmed as trained on the validated method. An OOS result from an untrained analyst may have a valid assignable cause - but this must be documented with evidence, not assumed. Training records must be checked at Step 3.

21 CFR 211.160(b)  ·  FDA OOS Guidance 2022 Section IV.A

Is an assignable laboratory cause identified with objective evidence?
"Analyst error" alone is NOT sufficient. Cause must be specific, documented and supported by objective evidence.
FDA OOS Guidance 2022 - what counts as assignable cause

"An assignable cause is one that can be identified and documented. Examples include: wrong dilution factor confirmed by pipette calibration records; expired reference standard confirmed by COA date; equipment malfunction confirmed by calibration records or maintenance log."

YES → Step 4 NO → Step 5 (Phase II)
↓ YES - assignable cause identified
4Document laboratory error with objective evidence
Assignable cause documented with specific objective evidence - not vague attribution. Supervisor and QA must both approve the invalidation before any retest. The Barr Laboratories decision established that laboratory error can invalidate an OOS result only when it is identified by a specific, documented, scientifically-valid cause.
⚠ Warning letter case - generic error attribution

2023 FDA warning letter to a generic manufacturer cited invalidation of 14 OOS results over 18 months attributed to "analyst error" without specific documented evidence for each invalidation. FDA found the pattern of invalidations without specific documented causes to be evidence of a systematic data integrity problem rather than legitimate laboratory error investigation.

Barr Laboratories Ltd v. Thompson (2003) - key principle

"Laboratory error may only be used to invalidate a test result if there is a scientifically documented reason to attribute the failure to error. A pattern of invalidations without documented specific causes may constitute evidence of CGMP violations."

FDA OOS Guidance 2022 Section IV.A.2  ·  Barr Laboratories precedent

4aConduct single retest - fresh sample preparation
One retest by a second qualified analyst. Fresh sample preparation. Same validated method. If retest is also OOS proceed to Phase II regardless of the laboratory error conclusion. Retesting to obtain a passing result - averaging, discarding the original or selecting only passing results - is a critical GMP violation.
⚠ Warning letter case - averaging passing and failing results

2024 warning letter cited a manufacturer who averaged initial OOS results with subsequent passing retest results to produce a passing average. FDA cited this as testing into compliance. All results - passing and failing - must be reported individually. The practice of averaging to pass is explicitly prohibited by the Barr Laboratories decision and FDA OOS Guidance.

5No laboratory cause found - escalate to Phase II
Phase I complete. No assignable laboratory error identified with objective evidence. Manufacturing investigation required. QA unit formally opens Phase II. Batch remains on quarantine hold pending Phase II completion.
✓ Best practice - Phase I documentation

Phase I report should be completed before Phase II begins. The report documents every element reviewed, the conclusion for each, and the reason no assignable laboratory cause was identified. A well-documented Phase I report that concludes no laboratory cause was found is just as important as one that identifies an error. It demonstrates rigour.

FDA OOS Guidance 2022 Section IV.B  ·  21 CFR 211.192
Phase II - Full Manufacturing Investigation Total investigation max 45 days
6QA formally opens Phase II investigation
Quality unit opens formal investigation record. Assigns investigation lead. Batch placed on quarantine hold. Manufacturing batch record retrieved. All relevant personnel identified. Investigation timeline documented from Phase I conclusion date. The 45-day total investigation clock includes Phase I time already elapsed.
21 CFR 211.192 - investigation scope

"The investigation shall extend to other batches of the same drug product and other drug products that may have been associated with the specific failure or discrepancy."

21 CFR 211.192  ·  ICH Q10 Section 3.2
↓
7Review complete batch production records
Complete batch record review covering: raw material COAs and test results, equipment calibration and cleaning records, in-process test results, deviation records, environmental monitoring data for the manufacturing period, personnel training records and any batch-specific observations or events noted during manufacture.
✓ Best practice - systematic record review

Use a structured review checklist with a sign-off field for each element reviewed. A checklist produces a documented audit trail showing every element was assessed. An investigator reviewing an open-narrative investigation report cannot readily confirm which records were actually reviewed versus which were assumed to be acceptable.

21 CFR 211.188  ·  21 CFR 211.192
↓
8Conduct root cause investigation with documented methodology
Apply documented RCA methodology: 5 Why for single-factor events, Ishikawa fishbone for complex multi-factor events. Human error is NOT an acceptable root cause - investigate why the system allowed the error to occur. Document the full analytical pathway from observed event to identified root cause.
⚠ Warning letter case - human error root cause

2025 warning letter cited a manufacturer whose OOS investigation programme assigned "operator error" or "analyst error" as the root cause in 34 of 47 OOS investigations over three years. FDA found this pattern demonstrated a systematic failure to identify true root causes. When the same OOS type recurred with different operators after retraining the original root cause assignment was demonstrated to have been incorrect.

ICH Q9(R1) 2023 - subjectivity reduction in RCA

"The use of structured risk management tools reduces reliance on individual judgement and improves consistency and reproducibility of investigations. The analytical pathway from observed event to root cause should be documented to allow independent verification of the conclusion."

ICH Q9(R1) 2023  ·  FDA OOS Guidance Section IV.B

YES → Step 9 NO → Extended investigation

9Additional testing if scientifically justified
If additional testing is needed to characterise the failure the number of additional tests must be pre-defined before testing begins. All results - passing and failing - must be reported. Averaging passing results while discarding failing results is prohibited. The protocol for additional testing must be documented and QA-approved before any additional samples are tested.
FDA OOS Guidance 2022 - Section V.B

"Any additional testing must have a pre-defined rationale and protocol. The number of tests to be conducted should be specified in advance. All test results, including those that are out of specification, must be reported and included in the investigation."

FDA OOS Guidance 2022 Section V.B  ·  Barr Laboratories decision
↓
10Implement CAPA with pre-defined effectiveness criteria
CAPA must address the identified root cause, not the symptom. Effectiveness criterion defined before implementation. Scheduled effectiveness check with measurable threshold. Scope extension to other batches documented per 21 CFR 211.192.
✓ Best practice - VOE design for OOS CAPAs

For OOS-related CAPAs the most meaningful effectiveness criterion is zero recurrences of the same OOS type in the same product, process step and analytical method within a defined monitoring period - typically 90 days. A retraining CAPA that defines its effectiveness criterion as training records completed misses the point. The measure of effectiveness is whether the OOS recurs, not whether training was documented.

21 CFR 211.192  ·  ICH Q10 Section 3.2.2  ·  FDA CAPA guidance
↓
11QA review and batch disposition decision
Quality unit reviews the complete investigation package. QA approves or rejects the investigation before batch disposition. No batch may be released or rejected before investigation completion and QA approval.
⚠ Warning letter case - premature disposition

2024 warning letter cited a manufacturer who released batches before OOS investigations were completed, documenting the release as provisional pending investigation conclusion. FDA found no regulatory basis for provisional release. A batch cannot be released before the investigation is complete. The quality unit approval of the investigation is a prerequisite for batch disposition, not a parallel activity.

⚠ Mandatory Scope Extension Under 21 CFR 211.192

Other batches of the same product Every other batch of the same drug product manufactured in the same period or using the same materials must be assessed. The scope extension conclusion must be documented even when no other batches are affected - the absence of impact is itself a required documented conclusion.
Other products associated with the same failure Other drug products associated with the same specific failure or discrepancy must be assessed. Equipment sharing, raw material supplier commonality and process step commonality are the primary assessment criteria for determining which other products require scope extension review.
Distributed batches 21 CFR 211.192 requires investigation whether or not the batch has already been distributed. If a distributed batch is associated with an OOS failure a field alert report or recall assessment may be required. The quality unit must assess distributed batch implications regardless of the commercial consequences of the conclusion.
21 CFR 211.192 - scope extension requirement

"The investigation shall extend to other batches of the same drug product and other drug products that may have been associated with the specific failure or discrepancy."

Never do this

Retest until a passing result is obtained then report only the passing result. Average passing and failing results. Attribute root cause to analyst error without specific objective documented evidence. Begin retesting before investigation is initiated. Release a batch before the investigation is QA-approved and complete.

Phase I timelines

Phase I must be completed within 3 to 10 business days. This is an FDA expectation from OOS Guidance 2022 - not a hard statutory deadline but a standard against which investigations are assessed. A Phase I that takes 30 days to conclude no laboratory cause is not compliant regardless of the conclusion. Document the reasons for any Phase I exceeding 10 business days.

Documentation standards

All raw data preserved with complete audit trail. Every decision point documented with dated rationale. All test results reported individually - passing and failing. Timestamps contemporaneous at each stage. QA approval documented at each phase transition. The investigation record must be self-contained - a reviewer should be able to follow every decision from the raw data without needing to ask the investigator for clarification.

Key enforcement reference

21 CFR 211.192 appeared 523 times in FDA warning letters FY2017 to FY2021 - consistently in the top five cited regulations. The most common specific citations: failure to extend investigation to other batches; inadequate investigation before retesting; failure to investigate discrepancies in distributed batches; and generic root cause attributions without documented evidence.
