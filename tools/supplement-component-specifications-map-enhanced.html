Identity Testing and Component Specifications - Confirming What Goes Into the Product

Under 21 CFR Part 111, a dietary supplement manufacturer must establish specifications for every component it uses and must verify, before use, that each component meets them. For a dietary ingredient, that includes at least one appropriate test or examination to confirm identity. Other specifications may be verified by testing, or by relying on a supplier certificate of analysis once the supplier has been qualified. This map details the pathway from setting specifications through sampling, testing, disposition, and records.

Component Types (Overview)

Dietary Ingredients

Components such as vitamins, minerals, herbs, and botanicals. Each lot requires at least one appropriate identity test or examination.

21 CFR 111.75(a)(1)

Other Components

Excipients, binders, fillers, and processing aids. Specifications may be confirmed by testing or a qualified COA.

21 CFR 111.75(a)(2)

Components That Fail

Rejected, or controlled through documented treatment or reprocessing approved by quality control before use.

21 CFR 111.77

Specification Categories (Overview)

Identity Confirms the component is what it is labeled and purchased to be, for example the correct plant part or species of a botanical.
Purity and composition Defines what the component should and should not contain, where these affect the quality of the finished supplement.
Strength Defines the amount of active constituent or marker expected, where this applies to the component.
Contaminant limits Sets limits for contaminants, such as heavy metals, microbes, or pesticide residues, that could adulterate the product.

Key Outputs (Overview)

Written specifications Documented identity and other specifications for every component, with approved methods.
Supplier qualification file Evidence that each supplier COA has been shown to be reliable, with periodic re-confirmation.
Test and examination records Results for each lot, linked to the sampling record and the quality control decision.
Disposition records Documented release, rejection, or controlled treatment decision for every component lot.

Component Control Quality Elements

Complete Specifications Every component has an identity specification and the other specifications that matter for the finished product.
Fit-for-Purpose Identity Test The identity method distinguishes the component from likely substitutes and adulterants.
Validated Methods Test methods are scientifically valid and suitable for the component matrix.
Representative Sampling Sampling plans reflect the lot and prevent a single container being treated as the whole lot.
COA Reliability Supplier COAs are accepted only after confirmation of results and periodic re-confirmation.
Quality Control Authority Quality control makes and documents the release and rejection decisions.
Failure Handling Failed components are quarantined and dispositioned under written procedures.
Record Traceability Records link each lot to its samples, results, and decisions from receipt to use.

Regulatory Text - Source Provisions

21 CFR 111.70(b) (paraphrased - refer to the official regulation for exact wording)

For each component used in manufacturing, a manufacturer must establish an identity specification and specifications for any limits on contaminants that may adulterate or lead to adulteration, and must establish other specifications, such as purity, strength, and composition, where necessary to ensure the dietary supplement meets its specifications.

21 CFR 111.75(a) (paraphrased - refer to the official regulation for exact wording)

Before use, a manufacturer must confirm the identity of each dietary ingredient by at least one appropriate test or examination, and must determine whether other component specifications are met, either by testing or by relying on a supplier certificate of analysis from a supplier the manufacturer has qualified.

21 CFR 111.77 (paraphrased - refer to the official regulation for exact wording)

A component that does not meet its specifications must not be used in manufacturing unless quality control personnel approve, with documentation, a treatment, in-process adjustment, or reprocessing that brings the component into compliance.

Common Inspection Findings - Component Identity and Specifications

Illustrative of recurring themes seen across published FDA Form 483s and warning letters. Not tied to a single specific citation.
No identity test performed on dietary ingredients Dietary ingredient lots are released on the supplier COA alone, with no identity test or examination by the manufacturer.
Supplier COA relied on without qualification No documented evidence that the COA results were confirmed, or that reliability is re-checked over time.
Specifications missing or incomplete Components used without written identity or contaminant specifications, or specifications that do not match the methods used.
Test methods not shown to be scientifically valid Methods used for identity or other tests have no support showing they can distinguish the component from likely adulterants.

Best Practice Guidance

Choose an identity method that can catch the likely substitute Pick a test that distinguishes the ingredient from the adulterants or lookalikes most likely in that supply chain.
Qualify suppliers with your own data Test the first lots independently against the COA and keep the comparison in the supplier file.
Re-confirm COAs on a schedule tied to risk Test more often for botanicals and high-risk ingredients, and less often for well-established suppliers.
Quarantine until quality control releases Keep every received lot segregated and labeled until the test results and the release decision are recorded.

Never do this

Release a dietary ingredient on a supplier COA alone with no identity test of your own. Accept a COA from a supplier you have never qualified. Use a component before quality control has approved it. Treat a failed lot as usable without a documented, approved treatment.

Dietary ingredient vs other component

A dietary ingredient lot needs at least one appropriate identity test or examination. For other specifications, and for other components, a qualified supplier COA can substitute for your own testing, provided the reliability conditions are met.

When a COA can be relied on

You qualify the supplier by confirming the COA results, the COA describes the tests or examinations conducted, you document the qualification, and you periodically re-confirm the results. A COA does not remove the need for an identity test on dietary ingredients.
