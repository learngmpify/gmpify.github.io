Dietary Supplement Specifications: When Looking Right Replaces Proving Identity

GMPify Training · November 2026 · 9 min read · Sources: FDA Warning Letters CMS #719302 and CMS #723021 | 21 CFR 111.70 | 21 CFR 111.205

A dietary supplement label makes a simple promise: what is on the label is what is in the bottle. Under 21 CFR Part 111, a manufacturer is responsible for being able to prove it. Two FDA warning letters issued in 2026, to a New York supplement manufacturer and a second New York powder manufacturer, show what happens when the proof is missing.

Both firms made products and sold them. What neither could show was that it had set specifications for those products and tested against them, so nobody could say whether a given batch contained the right ingredient, in the right amount, free of contamination.

FDA Warning Letter CMS #719302: Nature's Elements, Inc., April 7, 2026

FDA found that the firm had not established finished-product specifications for identity, purity, strength, and composition, and that its identity method, FTIR, lacked the specificity to identify the ingredients in multi-ingredient products.

What the two letters found

FDA inspected Nature's Elements in Bohemia, New York from October 14 to 16, 2025. Investigators found no finished-product specifications for identity, purity, strength, and composition across several products, and no specifications for product received from suppliers to confirm identity and consistency with purchase orders. The firm had also not prepared a master manufacturing record for each formulation and batch size, and had not prepared a batch production record for any of its finished products. The firm responded on November 5, 2025. FDA found the response inadequate: the documents lacked strength and purity specifications for each ingredient, and FDA stated that FTIR "lacks the specificity to identify the ingredients in multi-ingredient supplement products." Records submitted in support did not match, with different item numbers on different documents and no lot number recorded, which left FDA unable to evaluate the corrective action. The one master packaging record the firm provided as an example of future practice still omitted required elements.

On June 4, 2026, FDA issued a warning letter to Nutratech, LLC in Phoenix, New York, following an inspection from September 22 to October 15, 2025. The letter covered a ready-to-eat powdered supplement production room and found the same gap from a different direction. The firm had not set specifications for identity, purity, strength, and composition, and had relied on organoleptic testing alone. FDA's point was that these products contain many ingredients that may share the same organoleptic properties, so appearance, smell, and taste cannot confirm which ingredient is in the container. Revised specifications submitted for one product still did not include identity, purity, strength, and composition. The same inspection found hygiene failures around exposed powder, master manufacturing records missing written instructions for control steps, batch records missing equipment identity, cleaning dates, yields, and monitoring results, and product complaints that were not reviewed or investigated for specific lots.

Key fact

FDA did not object that the firms had tested nothing. It objected that what they had did not prove what it needed to prove. A visual check cannot establish identity in a blend, and one test method for a multi-ingredient product cannot stand in for specifications set ingredient by ingredient. Under 21 CFR 111.70, the specification comes first and the test has to be capable of checking it.

What 21 CFR Part 111 actually requires

21 CFR 111.70 requires you to establish specifications for components, in-process controls, finished batches, and packaging and labels. For finished batches, 111.70(e) calls for specifications for identity, purity, strength, and composition, and for limits on contaminants that may adulterate or lead to adulteration of the product. For components received from suppliers, 111.70(b) and related provisions require specifications that confirm identity, and 111.75 sets out how you determine whether those specifications are met, including by conducting at least one appropriate test or examination to verify identity of each dietary ingredient.

Part 111 then ties the specifications to documents. 21 CFR 111.205 requires a master manufacturing record for each unique formulation and batch size, 111.210 lists what the record must contain, 111.255 requires a batch production record each time a batch is made, and 111.260 lists what it must show. A product with no specification, no master record, and no batch record cannot demonstrate that any batch was made the way it was meant to be made.

What a defensible supplement specification programme contains

A Part 111 programme that survives inspection can show its work from the label to the batch:

  • Specifications written ingredient by ingredient. Identity, purity, strength, and composition are each set for the finished batch and for incoming components, with limits for contaminants.
  • Identity tests that can actually tell ingredients apart. The method is chosen for what it can separate. Where one test cannot distinguish the ingredients in a blend, the programme tests the components on receipt and has the rationale on file.
  • Organoleptic checks treated as screening, not proof. Appearance and smell are recorded, and they sit alongside an identity test that does the actual verification.
  • A master manufacturing record for every formulation and batch size. It contains the elements 111.210 lists, including written instructions for the control steps.
  • A batch record that matches the master record. Equipment, cleaning dates, actual and theoretical yields, and monitoring results are captured as the batch is made, and the record carries the lot number that ties it to the specification result.
  • Complaints and returns tied to lots. A complaint is reviewed against the batch it came from, and the investigation is recorded, as 111.553 and 111.560 require.

Our earlier posts on supplier qualification and change control cover two systems a supplement manufacturer relies on every time a supplier or formulation changes. A specification that is never revisited when an ingredient source changes stops describing the product being made.

Why a method has to match the product

The Nature's Elements response shows how this plays out in practice. The firm did have an identity method, and FDA still found it inadequate, because FTIR cannot identify individual ingredients in a multi-ingredient product. A method that is valid for a single-ingredient material can fail when the same technique is pointed at a blend. The question the specification has to answer is whether the test can tell the labeled ingredients apart from each other and from likely substitutes.

Why records and specifications fail together

Both letters found missing or incomplete master and batch records alongside the missing specifications, and that is not a coincidence. A specification only has value if it is written into the master record, checked in the batch record, and traceable to a lot. When the response documents in the Nature's Elements case carried mismatched item numbers and no lot number, FDA could not tell which batch the evidence belonged to. A record that cannot be tied to a lot cannot support a release decision.

When the production room is part of the evidence

The Nutratech inspection looked beyond paperwork. Exposed powder was being handled where employees' bare arms touched it, where residue sat on equipment above open product, and where cleanliness was recorded in batch records with generic statements. For a supplement that is eaten or stirred into a drink with no kill step, hygiene and equipment condition are part of the same question the specifications ask: can this batch be shown to be what it claims to be, free of what it should not contain. FDA also noted that the firm had submitted a corrective action plan without the evidence to show it had been carried out, and FDA said it would assess the corrective actions during the next inspection.

The bottom line

A dietary supplement manufacturer is expected to know what is in every batch it releases. Both 2026 letters came down to firms that could not show it, because the specification was never set, the test could not check it, or the record could not be tied to a lot. Under Part 111 the proof is part of the product, and FDA will ask to see it.
