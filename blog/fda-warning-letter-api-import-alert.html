FDA Warning Letter: API Supplier Loses US Market Access Under Import Alert 66-40

GMPify Training·August 25, 2026·5 min read·Sources: FDA Warning Letter, August 6 2026 | Import Alert 66-40 | 21 CFR Part 211 | FD&C Act §501(a)(2)(B)

On August 6, FDA issued a warning letter to a Chinese active pharmaceutical ingredient manufacturer following its review of the firm's response to an earlier records request. The consequence was immediate and severe: every drug and API from that facility is now blocked from entering the United States under Import Alert 66-40, imposed just over two weeks before the warning letter itself was published.

This breakdown covers what FDA actually cited, why the finding matters beyond the supplier itself, and what it signals for anyone responsible for qualifying and monitoring external manufacturers.

What FDA cited

The letter outlined deviations from current good manufacturing practice for APIs, covering the firm's manufacturing methods, facilities, and controls. FDA also flagged issues in the company's electronic drug listing submissions, a detail that's easy to overlook but signals investigators are cross-referencing regulatory filings against inspection findings, not just reviewing them in isolation.

Because the firm's methods, facilities, or controls for manufacturing, processing, packing, or holding of drugs do not conform to CGMP, its APIs are adulterated within the meaning of the Act.

Paraphrased from FD&C Act §501(a)(2)(B), as cited in the warning letter

That adulteration finding is a legal designation, not a claim that a specific batch caused harm. It means the company failed to demonstrate the controls were in place to reliably produce a compliant product, which is a lower bar for FDA to clear than proving actual contamination or defect, and a bar many companies underestimate until it's applied to a supplier they depend on.

Why this isn't just the supplier's problem

An import alert doesn't just stop the supplier's business. It stops anyone downstream who sources from that facility, mid-supply-chain, with little warning. Companies that qualified this vendor months or years ago now have to explain, to their own quality unit and potentially to their own regulators, how their oversight program missed the signs.

This is the exact scenario supplier qualification programs exist to catch early. Not after an import alert makes headlines, but during routine audits, periodic reassessments, and ongoing review of a supplier's own regulatory history.

The pattern worth noticing

FDA's action followed a records request under Section 704(a)(4), not a fresh on-site inspection. The firm had already been given a chance to respond in writing, and that response is what triggered the warning letter and the import alert. Paper responses to regulatory requests carry as much weight as what inspectors find on the floor.

What to check in your own supplier oversight

A few questions worth asking about your current qualification program:

  • Are you monitoring FDA's warning letter database for your active suppliers, or only reassessing at renewal intervals?
  • Does your risk assessment weight a supplier's prior inspection history, or only their most recent audit?
  • If a key supplier lost import eligibility tomorrow, how quickly would your quality unit know, and what's the documented contingency?

None of these are new questions. They're the same ones investigators ask when a company's own house is in order, and the same ones this firm's customers are almost certainly asking right now.

Who this matters to

Quality assurance managers, supplier quality engineers, procurement teams sourcing APIs or components internationally, and anyone responsible for maintaining a documented vendor qualification and monitoring program.
