Food Safety Plans: When the Hazard Analysis Misses the Hazard, or the Floor Ignores the Plan

GMPify Training · November 2026 · 9 min read · Sources: FDA Warning Letters CMS #721619 and CMS #727845 | 21 CFR 117.130 | 21 CFR 117.135

A food safety plan is only as good as two things: whether it names the right hazards, and whether the people on the floor do what it says. Two FDA warning letters issued in 2026, to a Baltimore nut processor and a Santa Clara bakery, each failed on one of those two tests.

Neither firm lacked a plan. One had a hazard analysis that missed a hazard sitting in the middle of its process. The other had controls on paper that employees did not follow, in a facility FDA had inspected before.

FDA Warning Letter CMS #721619: Barcelona Nut Company Inc., March 3, 2026

FDA found that the firm's hazard analysis for oil roasting did not identify allergen cross-contact as a hazard requiring a preventive control, and that its revised plan would not significantly reduce the risk.

What the two letters found

FDA inspected Barcelona Nut Company, a ready-to-eat nut, seed, candy, and trail mix manufacturer and repacker, from May 29 to June 26, 2025. Investigators found that the hazard analysis for oil roasting had not identified allergen cross-contact as a hazard. The firm responded in July 2025 with a wire-cloth filter for the roasting oil, and FDA found that the filter would not sufficiently remove peanut or tree nut proteins that may be present in the oil. FDA suggested replacing the oil between allergen products or using validated allergen-protein filtration. The letter went further. For peanut roasting, the firm's revised times and temperatures still did not ensure an adequate lethality treatment for Salmonella, and the firm supplied no validation data or scientific justification. FDA also noted that the hazard analysis did not address recontamination with environmental pathogens, even though ready-to-eat products remain exposed during cooling, and said it would expect a sanitation preventive control with robust environmental monitoring. The same inspection found a dead rodent, insects, and rodent excreta pellets "too numerous to count" on food-contact surfaces, and cashew residue left in a packaging line hopper before a switch to chocolate products.

About two months later, on May 12, 2026, FDA issued a warning letter to Dough-To-Go, Inc. in Santa Clara, California, following an inspection from October 29 to November 7, 2025. This firm's plan did identify the right hazards, including recontamination with environmental pathogens after baking and allergen cross-contact. The problem was execution. An employee wiped a scratched packing table with a frayed, ripped towel, then wiped gloved hands on the same towel and packed ready-to-eat products without changing gloves, and employees were not seen applying sanitizer after putting gloves on. Spilled nuts lay on a freezer floor beside cases of macadamia and almond, and some were still there on the last day of the inspection, at a facility that also handles wheat, eggs, milk, soy, and peanuts. FDA also cited a raw dough conveyor belt with frayed edges, a repeat observation from inspections in 2021 and 2018, a sanitizer rinse that tested at 0 ppm on a test strip, and heavy mouse activity recorded in a trap hidden by overgrown vegetation. The firm had not responded to the Form FDA 483 by the date of the letter, and FDA said it would evaluate corrective actions at its next inspection.

Key fact

The two letters sit at opposite ends of the same system. At Barcelona, the plan stopped short of the hazard, so no control existed to follow. At Dough-To-Go, the plan named the hazard, and the control was never carried out. FDA treats both as preventive controls failures under Part 117, and both make the product adulterated.

What 21 CFR Part 117 actually requires

21 CFR 117.130(a)(1) requires a hazard analysis to identify and evaluate known or reasonably foreseeable hazards for each type of food manufactured, processed, packed, or held, to determine whether any require a preventive control. The hazards include biological, chemical, and physical hazards, and allergen cross-contact and environmental pathogens on exposed ready-to-eat product both fall within them. 21 CFR 117.135 then requires preventive controls for each hazard that needs one, including process controls, food allergen controls, and sanitation controls, with 117.135(a)(1) obliging the facility to follow them. For a process control such as a roasting step that reduces Salmonella, the control has to be scientifically supportable, which is why FDA asked Barcelona for validation data.

The Part 117 requirements for current good manufacturing practice sit underneath all of this. Pest exclusion under 117.35(c), cleaning of food-contact surfaces under 117.35(d), and equipment maintenance under 117.40 are the conditions the preventive controls assume. A facility cannot rely on a sanitation control if the surfaces it protects have rodent evidence on them.

What a defensible food safety plan actually contains

A plan that survives inspection can show that it names the hazards and that people follow it:

  • A hazard analysis written for the process as it runs. Each step is walked through with the question of what could carry an allergen, a pathogen, or a foreign object into the product, including shared equipment such as roasting oil and packaging lines.
  • Controls validated against the hazard they claim to control. A filter, a roasting time, or a cleaning step is supported by data or scientific justification showing it actually works, and the justification is on file.
  • Environmental pathogen controls wherever product is exposed. Ready-to-eat product that is cooled, handled, or packed without a further kill step has a sanitation control and an environmental monitoring programme behind it.
  • Allergen changeover that is checked, not assumed. Residue is cleaned from hoppers, lines, and storage areas before an allergen-free product follows, and the check is recorded.
  • Monitoring that employees actually perform. Glove changes, sanitizer application, and sanitizer strength are verified on the floor, and a 0 ppm result is a deviation with a corrective action, not a number on a log.
  • Repeat observations treated as signals. A finding that appears at the next inspection, such as a frayed belt noted in 2018, 2021, and 2025, is escalated to the quality owner and closed with a verified fix.

Our earlier posts on supplier qualification and environmental monitoring cover two systems a food safety plan depends on every day. The monitoring principles carry over directly to ready-to-eat food, where an environmental pathogen is the hazard the plan has to keep out.

Why a corrective action has to match the hazard

The Barcelona response shows how this goes wrong after an inspection. The firm answered an allergen cross-contact finding by adding a wire-cloth filter, and FDA's reply was that the filter would not remove the peanut and tree nut proteins that matter. A fix that addresses the visible symptom without addressing the mechanism leaves the hazard in place and the plan still inadequate. The same pattern appeared on peanut roasting, where revised times and temperatures were offered without any validation behind them. A corrective action is a claim, and FDA expects the data that supports it.

Why a written control is not a working control

The Dough-To-Go letter makes the other half of the point. Its hazard analysis correctly flagged recontamination at post-bake steps, and the plan called for sanitation controls there. What FDA saw was a frayed towel, unchanged gloves, and missing sanitizer steps at exactly the point where the plan said controls were needed. A preventive control only protects the product when it happens every time, and that depends on training, supervision, equipment in good repair, and a monitoring step that someone actually reviews.

When the conditions underneath the plan fail

Both inspections found basic conditions that no preventive control can compensate for: rodent evidence on food-contact surfaces at Barcelona, and heavy mouse activity logged in an exterior trap hidden by vegetation at Dough-To-Go. FDA's concern was the same in each case. Sanitation and pest controls are what let every other control mean something. When they fail, the question of whether the plan is sound becomes secondary to whether the facility can be trusted to carry it out.

The bottom line

A food safety plan is a promise about hazards and a promise about behavior. Both 2026 letters came down to firms that could not keep one of those promises, either because the hazard was never named or because the control was never carried out. An inspector reads the plan first and then looks at the floor to see whether the two match.
