Stability Programs: When the Label Promises More Than the Data Can Prove

GMPify Training · October 2026 · 9 min read · Sources: FDA Warning Letters 320-26-112 and 320-26-61 | 21 CFR 211.166 | 21 CFR 211.137

An expiry date and a storage statement are two of the most quietly consequential claims on a drug label. They tell a patient how long a product can be trusted and under what conditions. Two FDA warning letters issued in 2026, to an over-the-counter and sterile products manufacturer in California and a contract manufacturer in Pennsylvania, show what happens when those claims are made without the stability data to back them.

Neither firm was missing a stability program on paper. The failures were in what the program could actually prove: methods that could not detect degradation, studies that supported one set of conditions while the label promised another, and failing stability results that were set aside without a reason.

FDA Warning Letter 320-26-112: K.C. Pharmaceuticals, Inc., August 12, 2026

FDA found that the firm had not established stability-indicating methods for the over-the-counter products it distributed in the U.S., and that its quality unit had not ensured stability testing followed the firm's own procedures.

What the two letters found

FDA inspected K.C. Pharmaceuticals in Pomona, California from January 20 to February 13, 2026. Investigators found that the firm had not established stability-indicating methods for its over-the-counter drug products. For one naphazoline HCl product, the assay validation protocol included a forced degradation study, but the validation report said that study would be performed later. FDA's position was that without forced degradation work to establish specificity, the accuracy of the assay results cannot be assured. The firm also missed stability time points, missed stability tests, or performed incorrect tests, and acknowledged a lack of proper quality unit oversight of the program. When the firm responded, FDA found the response inadequate because retrospective data had been reviewed using methods that were never validated as stability indicating, and because the firm had not assessed the potential for degradation products in in-expiry products already distributed.

About four months earlier, on April 9, 2026, FDA issued a warning letter to Medical Products Laboratories in Philadelphia, following an inspection from September 29 to October 16, 2025. The firm's own stability study supported a shorter expiration period and a narrower temperature range than the product carried. At its customer's instruction, the firm had labeled much wider storage conditions without adequate data to support them. In the same letter, FDA found that the firm had invalidated two subpotent assay results from stability testing, relying on resampling data with no identified laboratory error or rationale. The firm stated that it can no longer rely on written justifications from product owners to deviate from the general cGMP requirements.

Key fact

Both letters ended in the same required action: a stability program CAPA with stability-indicating methods, stability studies for each product in its marketed container closure system before distribution, representative batches added to the program every year, defined test attributes at each timepoint, and written procedures. FDA treats those as the baseline, not the ceiling.

What 21 CFR 211.166 actually requires

21 CFR 211.166(a) requires a written testing program designed to assess the stability characteristics of drug products, and that the results be used in determining appropriate storage conditions and expiration dates. The program has to include sample size and test intervals based on statistical criteria, storage conditions that reflect real handling, testing in the same container closure system the product is marketed in, and reliable, meaningful, and specific test methods. Where a drug is reconstituted before use, the program has to cover that state as well.

The phrase that matters most in both letters is "specific." A method that cannot separate the active ingredient from its degradation products can report a passing assay on a product that has degraded. 21 CFR 211.137 then ties the result to the label, requiring that the expiration date be related to storage conditions stated on the labeling, and that those conditions be supported by the stability data.

What a defensible stability program actually contains

A stability program that survives inspection treats the label as a claim that needs proof behind it:

  • Stability-indicating methods, proven before they are used. Forced degradation studies are completed as part of validation, not scheduled for later, so the method is shown to separate the active from its degradants.
  • Labeled conditions that match the data. The expiry date and storage statement come from the stability results for that product and container closure system. A customer's instruction or a justification from a product owner does not replace the data.
  • A pull schedule that is tracked as a commitment. Every time point, test, and attribute is scheduled, owned, and checked. A missed pull is a deviation with an impact assessment, not a gap that disappears into the next time point.
  • Stability failures handled as out-of-specification results. An invalidation needs an identified laboratory error and evidence for it. A resample that passes is not a reason.
  • Representative batches added every year. The ongoing program covers each product and container closure system, so the shelf life claim keeps being tested against current manufacturing.
  • Quality unit ownership of the whole program. The quality unit approves protocols, reviews results against the procedures, and confirms that stability findings reach the annual product review and, where needed, the market.

Our earlier posts on out-of-specification investigations and CAPA systems cover the two systems a stability failure should trigger. A subpotent stability result that is invalidated without evidence never reaches either one.

Why an unvalidated method puts every past result in question

The K.C. Pharmaceuticals response shows how far this problem reaches. The firm offered a retrospective review of its stability data, but the review relied on the same methods that had not been shown to be stability indicating. That makes the review unable to answer the question FDA was actually asking, which is whether degradation was occurring in products still within expiry on the U.S. market. When a method cannot be trusted, the data it produced cannot be used to defend the batches, and the remediation has to start with the method before it can move on to the product.

Why the label is a claim, not a convenience

The Medical Products Laboratories letter makes the other half of the point. A contract manufacturer is often asked to label a product the way the product owner wants. In this case the firm's own data supported a shorter expiry and a narrower temperature range, and the label said otherwise. FDA's reasoning was direct: without the appropriate stability studies, there is no adequate scientific evidence for the wider conditions. Contract arrangements can allocate responsibilities, but they do not change what the stability data shows, and the firm releasing the product remains answerable for the claim on it.

When failing results disappear

The same Medical Products Laboratories inspection found that two subpotent assay results from stability testing were invalidated on the strength of resampling data alone. Stability OOS results carry extra weight because they speak to every batch of the product already in the market. FDA also noted that the firm had not reviewed other products for improperly invalidated results. A stability failure that is explained away protects one batch record and leaves the shelf life claim unsupported for all of them.

The bottom line

A stability program is the evidence behind two statements on every label: how long the product lasts and how it must be kept. Both 2026 letters came down to programs that could not show their work, whether through a method that could not detect degradation, a label wider than the data, or a failing result with no reason behind its removal. An inspector will check whether the label can be traced to the data.
